01FPS Mission & The PSO Role

The Federal Protective Service (FPS) is a federal law enforcement agency under the Department of Homeland Security (DHS). Its mission is to protect federal facilities, the people who work in and visit them, and the property within them.

FPS itself employs about 900 sworn federal law enforcement officers. To cover the roughly 9,000+ federal facilities it's responsible for, FPS contracts with private security firms — like Kerberos — who employ approximately 13,000 Protective Security Officers (PSOs) nationwide.

What a PSO IS

  • A trained private security professional working under a federal contract.
  • The visible, front-line security presence at a federal facility.
  • The "eyes and ears" of FPS — responsible for observing, reporting, and responding to incidents per post orders.
  • Authorized to perform access control, screening, patrol, and emergency response on the assigned facility.

What a PSO IS NOT

  • NOT a federal law enforcement officer. PSOs do not derive arrest authority from 40 USC § 1315 — that statute grants LE authority to sworn FPS officers and agents, not contract PSOs.
  • NOT a sworn peace officer. Your authority to detain is the same as any private citizen, supplemented by state law and your post orders.
  • Impersonating a federal officer can result in criminal charges under 18 USC § 912. Identify yourself as a PSO. Never claim to be a federal agent, marshal, or police officer.
Core test concept: The PSO's role is "Observe and Report." You deter through presence, detect threats early, delay intruders, and respond per post orders — but you summon FPS or local law enforcement to investigate and arrest.

02PSO Authority — and Its Limits

This is one of the most heavily tested topics on the PSOTP exam. Get clear on the difference between FPS sworn authority and PSO contract authority.

40 USC § 1315 — FPS Officer Authority (NOT PSOs)

This statute grants sworn FPS officers and agents the power to:

  • Enforce federal laws and regulations protecting persons and property.
  • Carry firearms.
  • Make arrests without a warrant for any offense against the United States committed in their presence, or for any felony with reasonable grounds.
  • Serve warrants and subpoenas.
  • Conduct investigations.
Critical exam point: A contract PSO is NOT one of the "officers or agents" designated under 40 USC § 1315. Your authority to detain comes from state law (citizen's arrest, shopkeeper's privilege, defense of property), supplemented by the property owner's authority delegated through your post orders.

What a PSO Can Do

  • Control access to the facility per post orders.
  • Conduct administrative searches at screening checkpoints (bags, magnetometer).
  • Deny entry to anyone refusing screening or violating facility rules.
  • Temporarily detain a person for safety reasons, or for a crime committed in your presence, until law enforcement arrives.
  • Use reasonable force to defend self, others, or — in limited cases — property.
  • Direct people to leave federal property when their presence violates 41 CFR or post orders.

What a PSO Cannot Do

  • Make arrests in the same sense a sworn officer does (no booking, no fingerprinting, no transport to jail).
  • Initiate vehicle pursuits off the property.
  • Conduct criminal investigations.
  • Serve warrants or subpoenas.
  • Use deadly force to stop fleeing suspects who don't pose a deadly threat.
  • Use force to enforce traffic laws or routine ordinance violations.

03Three Types of Federal Jurisdiction

The jurisdictional status of your assigned facility determines which agency has primary law enforcement authority. You must know which type applies to your post. It's in your post orders.

Exclusive
The federal government has sole authority. State and local police have no general police powers inside the facility's footprint. Federal LE handles all crimes.
Concurrent
Federal and state/local authorities share jurisdiction equally. Either may enforce criminal laws. Most common type at modern federal buildings.
Proprietary
The federal government has property rights only (it's a tenant or owner) but the state retains all law enforcement authority. Local police handle crimes.

How to Tell Which You're On

  • Check your post orders. The jurisdictional status is stated as part of the facility's contract documents.
  • Pre-1940 federal land acquisitions often default to Exclusive jurisdiction unless ceded back. After the Buck Act and related legislation, the federal government must affirmatively accept jurisdiction.
  • Leased space in a private commercial building is almost always Proprietary.
  • Many large federal complexes (courthouses, agency HQs) are Concurrent.

Why It Matters

If a felony occurs in front of you, your call differs based on jurisdiction:

  • Exclusive: Notify FPS MegaCenter and FPS responds. Local police generally cannot enter to act.
  • Concurrent: Notify FPS first; either FPS or local can respond.
  • Proprietary: Local police handle it. Notify FPS for situational awareness.
Default rule for the exam: When in doubt, notify the FPS MegaCenter. They route the response to the appropriate agency.

0441 CFR Part 102-74 — Conduct on Federal Property

This is the federal regulation that governs what people may and may not do inside federal buildings. The PSO enforces these rules. Know the major categories.

Prohibited Items

  • Firearms and other dangerous weapons (except for authorized law enforcement and military personnel).
  • Explosives or items intended for use as explosives or incendiaries.
  • Controlled substances — including marijuana, which remains illegal under federal law regardless of state medical or recreational laws.

Prohibited Conduct

  • Disturbances — loitering, disorderly conduct, creating a nuisance, or disrupting official business.
  • Gambling — except specifically authorized blind-vendor lotteries.
  • Alcoholic beverages — unless authorized for official events.
  • Smoking — except where explicitly permitted by agency regulations (most interiors prohibit it).
  • Unauthorized soliciting, vending, debt collection, or commercial advertising.
  • Unauthorized posting or distributing of materials on walls, bulletin boards, or other surfaces.
  • Photography for news, advertising, or commercial purposes inside the building requires authorization — though news media may photograph in entrance lobbies and at public press events under standard rules.
  • Animals — only service animals assisting persons with disabilities, and official working K9s, are allowed. Personal pets are not.

Authorized Conduct

  • Inspection of packages and bags entering federal property — this is established by regulation and by signage. Refusal to consent means denied entry, not arrest.
  • Peaceful First Amendment activity on exterior sidewalks and plazas — provided it does not block ingress/egress or disrupt building operations.
  • Breastfeeding is explicitly protected by federal law on federal property in any location where the woman and child are otherwise authorized to be (Public Law 110-181, § 629).

05Federal Crimes Against PSOs & Employees

18 USC § 111 — Assault on a Federal Officer or Employee

This statute makes it a federal crime to forcibly assault, resist, oppose, impede, intimidate, or interfere with a federal officer or employee — or anyone assisting them — while engaged in their official duties. Penalty tiers:

Simple Assault
No contact, threats, or intimidation — up to 1 year in federal prison.
Contact / Felony Intent
Physical contact or assault with intent to commit another felony — up to 8 years.
Deadly Weapon / Injury
Use of a deadly weapon or infliction of bodily injury — up to 20 years.

18 USC § 1114 — Who Is Protected

This section defines who is covered under § 111 protections: any officer or employee of the United States or any federal agency, including uniformed services, OR any person assisting such an officer or employee in the performance of their official duties.

Key takeaway for PSOs: When you're on duty performing your contracted security functions, you are "a person assisting" federal officers and employees in the protection of federal property. An assault on you while you're on post can be prosecuted federally under § 111.

Common Federal Crimes on FPS Property

  • Theft of government property — 18 USC § 641.
  • Destruction of government property — 18 USC § 1361.
  • Trespass on federal property — typically charged via 41 CFR violation citation.
  • Impersonating a federal officer — 18 USC § 912. (Yes, this applies if a PSO falsely claims federal LE status.)
  • Threats against federal officials — various statutes.

06Use of Force Doctrine

The Legal Standard: Graham v. Connor (1989)

The Supreme Court established that use of force is evaluated under the "objective reasonableness" standard — judged from the perspective of a reasonable officer on the scene at the moment force was used, not in 20/20 hindsight. Factors considered:

  • The severity of the crime or threat.
  • Whether the subject poses an immediate threat to officers or others.
  • Whether the subject is actively resisting or attempting to evade.

Use of Force Continuum

  1. Officer Presence — visible uniformed deterrence.
  2. Verbal Direction — clear, calm, lawful commands.
  3. Empty-Hand Control — escorts, holds, restraint techniques.
  4. Less-Lethal Options — OC spray, baton (where issued and trained).
  5. Deadly Force — firearm. Reserved for imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury.

Deadly Force Justification — Three Elements

All three must be present:
  1. Ability — the subject has the capability to cause death or serious physical injury.
  2. Opportunity — the subject is positioned to use that ability right now.
  3. Jeopardy — the subject's actions indicate intent to act on it.

Force Must Be

  • Reasonable under the circumstances known at the time.
  • Necessary — no lesser option would have worked.
  • Proportional to the threat.
  • Stopped the moment the threat is neutralized or compliance is achieved.
Never use deadly force to: protect property alone, stop a fleeing suspect who poses no deadly threat, or as punishment. Warning shots are prohibited.

07Detention vs. Arrest

Detention
A brief, temporary hold of a person based on reasonable suspicion of misconduct, to maintain safety or wait for law enforcement. Limited in scope and duration. PSOs do this regularly.
Arrest
Taking a person into custody with the intent to deliver them to the criminal justice system. PSOs do not perform arrests in the same sense as sworn officers; what they do is sometimes called a "citizen's arrest" and is legally a detention until LE arrives.

If You Detain Someone

  1. Use only the force necessary to safely effect the detention.
  2. Notify the FPS MegaCenter and request law enforcement response.
  3. Inform the person why they are being detained.
  4. Hold them only as long as reasonably necessary for LE to arrive.
  5. Document everything — time, location, observations, witnesses, statements.
  6. Transfer custody to responding officers as soon as they arrive.

Civil Liability Risks

If you detain someone wrongly — wrong person, no actual offense, excessive force, prolonged detention — you and your employer can face:

  • False imprisonment claims.
  • Battery or assault claims.
  • Civil rights violations (especially if you were acting at LE direction).
  • Negligence claims.

The Fourth Amendment generally restricts government searches. As a contract PSO performing security functions on federal property, your screening searches are administrative — based on implied consent (you can't enter without consenting to screening). But if you act at the direction of law enforcement, you become a "state actor" and Fourth Amendment protections kick in.

08Administrative Search & Screening

The Legal Basis

Screening at federal facilities is an administrative search. Entry is conditioned on consent to screening. A person who refuses screening is denied entry — they are not arrested for the refusal itself.

Walk-Through Magnetometer (Metal Detector)

  • Detects concealed metallic objects.
  • If an alarm sounds: ask the person to remove and divest pocket items, then have them step back through. If it alarms again, use a hand-held wand for targeted screening.
  • Repeated alarms or refusal to cooperate = denied entry.

X-Ray (Bag Screener)

  • Used to screen carried items for prohibited contents.
  • If a suspected explosive or IED appears on the monitor: stop the belt immediately, do not touch the bag, isolate the area, evacuate as appropriate, notify the MegaCenter, and request response.
  • Never run a suspected device through again "to be sure."

Common Prohibited Items Found in Screening

  • Firearms (unless properly credentialed).
  • Knives over allowable length, fixed-blade knives.
  • Pepper spray, mace, stun guns, tasers.
  • Multi-tools with locking blades.
  • Tools that could be used as weapons (hammers, large screwdrivers).
  • Marijuana and other controlled substances.

09Post Orders & Standard Operating Procedures

Post orders are your operational bible. They are written, contract-mandated documents that specify exactly what your duties are at a given post.

Typical Post Order Contents

  • Facility jurisdiction type and key tenant agencies.
  • Specific patrol routes and timing.
  • Access control procedures — who can enter, what credentials, what screening.
  • Emergency response procedures — fire, bomb threat, medical, active shooter.
  • Specific prohibitions and restricted areas.
  • Reporting requirements and chain of notification.
  • Contact numbers — FPS MegaCenter, building manager, local police.
  • Required equipment and uniform standards.

Rules for Post Orders

  • Read them at the start of every shift. They change. Updates are issued.
  • Follow them precisely. Deviation is grounds for removal from contract.
  • If a post order seems to conflict with law, training, or safety — notify your supervisor and the MegaCenter; do not ignore it unilaterally.
  • Post orders are facility-specific. What's authorized at one post may be prohibited at another.

10FPS MegaCenter & Communication

The FPS MegaCenter is the 24/7/365 communications and dispatch center that supports all FPS operations and contract PSOs. There are MegaCenters in different regions; calls route based on your area code.

National Number
1-877-437-7411
Hours
24 hours / 7 days / 365 days
Function
Dispatch, incident logging, LE coordination, status checks

When You Call the MegaCenter

  • Identify yourself: name, employer (Kerberos), post location.
  • State the nature of the call: emergency vs. routine, type of incident.
  • Describe location precisely — building, floor, room, entrance.
  • Describe persons involved using standard format (sex, race, height, clothing, etc.).
  • Stay on the line until the dispatcher releases you.

Radio Discipline

  • Use plain language. Avoid codes unless the post orders specify them.
  • Press the talk button, pause briefly, then speak. Otherwise the first word gets cut off.
  • Speak slowly and clearly. Spell unusual names phonetically (Alpha, Bravo, Charlie…).
  • Never broadcast sensitive information (suspect's full Social Security number, etc.).
  • Acknowledge transmissions: "Copy," "10-4," "Received."

11Report Writing

Every significant incident, breach, medical event, or use of force gets documented in an incident report. These reports become legal evidence — sometimes years later. Write them like they'll be read in court, because they might be.

The Six W's

  • Who — all persons involved (names, DOB, addresses, descriptions).
  • What — what happened, in chronological order.
  • When — date and 24-hour time.
  • Where — exact location.
  • Why — observed motive or cause (if any).
  • How — method, sequence, tools/weapons used.

Report Writing Rules

  • Facts, not opinions. Don't write "the man was angry." Write "the man was shouting, his face was red, and his fists were clenched."
  • First person, past tense, active voice. "I observed…" not "It was observed that…"
  • Use direct quotes for statements: He stated, "I'll be back."
  • No slang, no jargon the reader might not understand.
  • Never alter a submitted report — add a supplemental report instead.
  • Sign and date the report. Note witnesses who were present.

12Emergency Response

Fire — RACE

  • Rescue anyone in immediate danger.
  • Alarm — pull the alarm, call 911, notify the MegaCenter.
  • Contain — close doors to limit spread.
  • Extinguish (only if small, contained, and you're trained) or Evacuate.

Fire Extinguisher — PASS

  • Pull the pin.
  • Aim at the base of the fire.
  • Squeeze the handle.
  • Sweep side to side.

Medical Emergency

  • Call 911 first, then notify MegaCenter.
  • Render aid within your training (CPR, AED, bleeding control, Stop the Bleed).
  • Use universal precautions — assume all bodily fluids are infectious. Gloves and barriers.
  • Stay with the victim until EMS arrives. Document what you observed and what aid you rendered.

Bomb Threat (Phone Call)

  • Keep the caller on the line as long as possible. Listen for accent, background noise, voice characteristics.
  • Ask: When will it go off? Where is it? What does it look like? Why? Who are you?
  • Do not hang up. Signal a co-worker to notify supervisor and the MegaCenter.
  • If a device is identified or suspected: do not touch it, do not move it, do not use radios near it (RF can detonate some triggers). Evacuate per post orders.

Evacuation

  • Direct occupants to nearest safe exits per the building's emergency plan.
  • Account for persons with disabilities — assist or designate assistance.
  • Establish perimeter at assembly point. Keep evacuees clear of the building.
  • Provide situational reports to MegaCenter and responding agencies.

13Active Threats & WMD/CBRNE

Active Shooter Response

Federal guidance is RUN · HIDE · FIGHT in that order — but as an armed PSO, your specific response is dictated by your post orders and FPS protocol. The DHS guidance is the baseline framework:

  • Run — evacuate if a safe path exists. Leave belongings. Help others escape if possible.
  • Hide — out of sight, lock and barricade doors, silence phones.
  • Fight — as a last resort, with total commitment.

As an armed PSO at an FPS post, your protocol may direct you to address the threat to protect life. Know your post-specific active threat protocol cold. It is in your post orders.

CBRNE Awareness

Chemical
Unusual odors (almonds, garlic, bleach), dead vegetation or animals, casualties with similar symptoms in the same area. Get upwind and uphill.
Biological
Usually invisible. First sign is often an unexplained cluster of illness. Isolate suspicious substances (powders, liquids).
Radiological
"Dirty bomb" — conventional explosive disperses radioactive material. Distance, time, and shielding are your defenses.
Nuclear
Full nuclear device. Maximum distance, take cover from initial flash, shelter in place against fallout afterward.
Explosive
Most common CBRNE incident. See bomb threat procedures.

Suspicious Package / Mail Indicators

  • Excessive postage or hand-stamped postage.
  • No return address or unfamiliar return address.
  • Misspelled words, poor handwriting.
  • Oily stains, discoloration, protruding wires or strings.
  • Restrictive markings: "Personal," "Confidential," "Do Not X-Ray."
  • Lopsided, uneven, or unusual weight for size.
  • Strange odors.

If suspicious: Do not move it. Do not open it. Do not put it in water. Isolate the area, evacuate as needed, notify supervisor and the MegaCenter. Do not use radios within close proximity.

14Crime Scene Preservation

When a crime has occurred on your post, your job is to preserve the scene for the responding investigators, not to investigate it yourself.

Five Steps

  1. Secure the scene. Establish a perimeter. Keep everyone out — including curious employees and supervisors.
  2. Protect evidence. Don't touch, move, or alter anything unless absolutely necessary for safety (e.g., turning off running water).
  3. Identify and separate witnesses. Get names and contact info. Keep witnesses apart so they don't influence each other's accounts.
  4. Note your observations. Time of arrival, what you saw, who was present, who entered the scene, what changes occurred.
  5. Brief the responding investigators. Hand off custody of the scene and provide your initial observations.
Do NOT: Move bodies (unless absolutely needed for medical aid). Move weapons. Pick up shell casings. Clean up. Take photos for personal use. Discuss the scene on social media. Allow unauthorized personnel into the scene.

15Conduct, Ethics & Appearance

On-Duty Conduct

  • Honesty in all reports and statements. A single falsified report ends your career and may bring criminal charges.
  • No alcohol or impairing substances on duty or within 8 hours before a shift. Subject to random drug testing.
  • No sleeping on post. Documented and photographed in the past — instant removal.
  • No abandoning post without authorization and relief.
  • Carry your DPSST license and any FPS-issued credentials at all times. Present to law enforcement on request.
  • Discretion — never disclose post information, security procedures, schedules, or tenant information to unauthorized persons or on social media.

Uniform & Appearance

  • Uniform clean, pressed, and complete per contract specifications.
  • Footwear: black, plain, closed-toed, with black laces. Heels under 1 inch. No athletic shoes, sandals, suede, or decorative styling.
  • Outerwear must be FPS-approved.
  • Metal breast badge on left side when shirt is outermost garment.
  • Hair, grooming, and personal appearance per contract standards.

Interacting with the Public

  • Treat every person professionally regardless of their behavior toward you.
  • Be friendly but firm. Especially with children — they may be curious about your gear; balance approachability with maintaining control of your equipment.
  • No sarcasm, profanity, or unprofessional language.
  • Sexual harassment of or by anyone is prohibited and reportable.
  • Be alert to your surroundings — vigilance is your best defense against assault.
